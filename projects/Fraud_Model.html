Does the Fraud Alert Score Actually Predict Productivity?

A data-driven evaluation of a fraud alert generation framework operating under extreme class imbalance—combining operational trend analysis with statistical modeling to determine whether the existing alert score meaningfully separates productive from non-productive alerts.

Key finding: the alert score contained predictive signal, but its discriminatory power was limited—highlighting opportunities for additional features, score refinement, and threshold optimization.

S

Situation

The fraud alert generation framework was producing a high volume of alerts, but only a small proportion ultimately resulted in productive outcomes.

Approximately 97% of alerts were non-productive, while only approximately 3% were productive.

The core business question: Was the low productivity simply an inherent characteristic of the alert population, or was the underlying alert score successfully prioritizing alerts that were more likely to become productive?

T

Task

I structured the analysis around two questions:

01 · Operational Stability

Was alert generation stable over time, with productivity rates remaining consistently low rather than being driven by a temporary period?

02 · Predictive Effectiveness

Did the existing alert score provide meaningful predictive information about whether an alert would become productive?

A

Action

1. Established the operating baseline

  • Analyzed historical alert-level data containing alert status, alert score, productivity outcome, and population group.
  • Analyzed alert distributions across population groups using histograms.
  • Evaluated monthly alert volumes to understand changes in alert-generation patterns.
  • Compared productive, non-productive, and open alerts.
  • Evaluated productivity trends over the prior year and found productivity remained relatively stable at approximately 2–3%.

2. Decomposed productive outcomes

Rather than treating every productive alert as identical, I examined the composition of the productive population.

  • Approximately 90% of productive alerts had an RFI (Request for Information) status.
  • Further analyzed productive outcomes, including RFIs and alerts escalated for case creation.

3. Tested the predictive value of alert score

I built a logistic regression model to evaluate whether the alert score could predict alert productivity.

Model specification

  • Target: Productive vs. non-productive
  • Predictor: Alert score
  • Model: Logistic regression
  • Evaluation: AUC-ROC and confusion-matrix metrics

Addressing class imbalance

The original population was extremely imbalanced, with productive alerts representing only a small fraction of observations.

I therefore used upsampling of the productive class to create a balanced modeling dataset containing 59,270 observations.

The held-out test set contained 29,635 observations.

4. Evaluated model performance beyond accuracy

What does the 0.61 AUC actually mean?

The alert score is not useless. The statistically significant relationship indicates that higher alert scores are associated with a greater likelihood of productivity.

However, an AUC of 0.61 indicates only modest discriminatory ability.

Put simply: if one productive and one non-productive alert were randomly selected, the score would rank the productive alert higher approximately 61% of the time.

Therefore, the score contains predictive signal, but it is not a strong standalone predictor of productivity.

R

Result

The analysis demonstrated that the alert-generation framework was operationally stable, with productivity consistently remaining around 2–3%.

The predictive analysis also showed that the alert score was statistically informative—higher scores were associated with greater likelihood of productivity.

The important limitation

With an AUC of 0.61, the existing alert score had limited discriminatory power. It should therefore not be characterized as a highly effective standalone predictor of productivity.

Business / Model Implications

  • Feature enhancement: investigate additional variables that may better distinguish productive from non-productive alerts.
  • Score refinement: evaluate whether the existing scoring framework can be improved using additional predictive information.
  • Threshold optimization: evaluate thresholds based on the operational trade-off between missed productive alerts and unnecessary investigations.
  • Ongoing monitoring: continue tracking productivity and discriminatory performance over time.
Model-validation takeaway: The analysis did not simply ask whether the model “worked.” It quantified where the scoring framework had predictive value, where it fell short, and what areas warranted further investigation.

The Story in One Sentence
